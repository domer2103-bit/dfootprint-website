# AI Prescription™

> **Layer:** Knowledge & Methodology | **Purpose:** The named diagnose-then-prescribe methodology at the centre of Digital Footprint's positioning | **Status:** Active — source of truth | **Owner:** Founder | **Version:** 1.1

We do not sell AI tools. We diagnose business problems and prescribe the simplest improvements that return time, attention and capability to the business. This document is the repeatable process behind that promise — designed to work consistently across thousands of businesses, not just the ones the founder personally meets.

## The Core Sequence

Awareness → Business Health Check (hypothesis) → Diagnosis (reality) → AI Suitability Test → Prioritisation → Prescription (roadmap) → Implementation (optional, separate decision) → Outcome Measurement → Reflection → Methodology Improvement.

## 1. Before We Meet: The Business Health Check

A lightweight, AI-assisted intake — not a long traditional questionnaire. The business owner provides information naturally: simple questions, voice responses, descriptions of frustrations, relevant documents or examples. An AI organises this into a structured overview but draws no conclusions.

**Output of this stage:** business context, possible friction points, initial assumptions, questions to investigate.

**Explicitly not the output:** recommendations, solutions, or a diagnosis.

**Governing principle:** "The intake creates a hypothesis. The diagnosis discovers the reality." Self-reported information is a starting hypothesis to investigate, never a conclusion to act on — business owners are often the worst source for identifying their own biggest friction, because habitual problems become invisible ("that's just how we do it here").

## 2. The Diagnosis: A Skeptical, Paid, Standalone Stage

The diagnosis is a distinct process, separate from any build or implementation work, and it is paid for on its own — not bundled into a sales conversation. Its purpose is not to confirm what the client believes. Its purpose is to test it.

The consultant's role here is closer to a doctor than a salesperson.

**Questions the diagnosis must ask of every hypothesis from the Health Check:**

Is this really the root problem? What evidence supports this? What assumptions are we making? What happens if we do nothing? Is AI actually the right intervention? Is there a simpler non-AI solution?

**Why the diagnosis is paid, independent of what happens next:** an independent diagnosis has value even when the outcome is "do not use AI" or "do not work with Digital Footprint." All of the following are successful outcomes, because the client walks away with real clarity:

1. Digital Footprint implements the solution.
2. The client implements the recommendation themselves.
3. The client needs a different solution outside our expertise.
4. The correct decision is to wait or do nothing.

(Note: where a diagnosis surfaces multiple friction points, each one is assigned its own outcome from this list — see section 5, "Outcomes happen per friction point, not only per engagement.")

**Trust principle:** "We do not diagnose problems in order to sell solutions. We sell diagnosis because clarity has value, and solutions should come after understanding." This is positioned to the client as an AI Prescription Assessment or Business Health Check, not a sales consultation, and the client is told upfront that the diagnosis may conclude they shouldn't hire Digital Footprint any further.

**Multi-perspective diagnosis.** A business cannot be fully diagnosed from one perspective. Where possible, the diagnosis should include multiple viewpoints: the owner/founder perspective, the operational/team perspective, and the customer-facing perspective where relevant. The person responsible for the business may not see friction that has become normalised — problems that are obvious to whoever does the daily work, or to whoever deals with customers directly, are often invisible to the person who commissioned the assessment. Where the business is too small for multiple perspectives to be practical, this should be noted as a limitation of the diagnosis, not silently skipped.

**Client requests are hypotheses, not prescriptions.** A requested solution receives the same scrutiny as an identified problem. A client asking for AI automation does not prove AI is the correct intervention — it proves the client believes it is, which is itself just another hypothesis to test. The diagnosis must investigate why they believe this solution is needed, what underlying problem they are actually trying to solve, and whether another intervention would create better results. A specific, named request should never be scoped and delivered at face value without first being run through this same skepticism.

## 3. The AI Suitability Test: Deciding Whether AI Should Be Involved At All

Governing principle: "The first question is not where AI can help. The first question is whether AI should be involved."

AI is rejected as the solution when one or more of these conditions are present:

**The process is not understood.** If the business cannot clearly explain the current workflow, automating it would only make confusion faster. The first intervention may need to be process improvement, not AI.

**The task depends heavily on human judgement or carries significant consequences.** AI can support these situations, but should not replace accountable human decisions where context, empathy or responsibility matter.

**The frequency or volume is too low.** The potential time saved does not justify the complexity, cost or maintenance of building an AI solution.

**The foundations are not ready.** Poor data, unclear ownership, broken processes or unsuitable systems mean AI will not solve the underlying problem — it will automate the dysfunction.

**The risk outweighs the benefit.** Even if something can be automated, ask whether the consequences of a mistake are acceptable.

This test must be applied consistently, by the founder today and by any future consultant working under this methodology, so that different people reach the same conclusion given the same evidence.

## 4. Prioritisation: Sequencing, Not Just Sizing

Governing principle: "We do not prescribe the biggest possible intervention. We prescribe the most appropriate next step."

ROI matters, but the single biggest financial opportunity is not automatically the correct first move. Once multiple opportunities have passed the AI Suitability Test, rank them against all of the following, not ROI alone:

**Impact** — how much time, money, quality or customer experience improvement could this create? (Use ROI_Calculator.md for the operational component of this.)

**Effort and complexity** — how difficult is it to implement, maintain and support?

**Business readiness** — does the client have the processes, data and systems needed for this to succeed?

**Trust-building value** — especially for a new client, does this create confidence and demonstrate value quickly?

**Dependency value** — does solving this unlock other improvements later?

**Risk** — what happens if this fails? Is this appropriate as a first implementation?

The goal is the best sequence of improvements, not the largest single win.

## 5. The Prescription: A Roadmap, Not a List

The deliverable from the diagnosis and prioritisation stages is a roadmap, not a set of ideas. A good prescription explains:

What should happen first, and why this is the right starting point. What should happen later. What should not be done yet, and why.

This roadmap references the Automation Catalog for anything that matches an existing product, and flags anything genuinely novel as a candidate for a new catalogue entry.

**Outcomes happen per friction point, not only per engagement.** A single business diagnosis will usually surface more than one friction point, and each one receives its own recommended outcome rather than the whole engagement being classified as a single success or failure. The possible outcomes per friction point are: implement with Digital Footprint, client implements internally, process improvement without AI, referral to another specialist, or monitor and revisit later. It is normal and expected for a single prescription to contain a mix of these — for example, one friction point built by Digital Footprint, another handed back to the client as a process fix, and a third flagged as outside Digital Footprint's expertise entirely. The goal is not to classify the whole engagement as success or failure; the goal is the right outcome for each individual friction point.

## 6. Implementation: A Separate Decision

Implementation only happens after the prescription exists, and only if the client chooses it — by Digital Footprint, by the client themselves, or by someone else entirely. The prescription must stand on its own value regardless of who ends up doing the work.

## 7. Measuring Success: Three Levels

The ROI Calculator's time/money figure is necessary but not sufficient. Success is assessed at three levels:

**Level 1 — Operational Impact.** Did the intervention create measurable improvement? Time saved, cost reduced, errors reduced, faster response times, improved consistency.

**Level 2 — Human Impact.** Did the returned capacity create meaningful improvement? What did the client do with the time returned? Did they spend more time on important work? Did customer relationships improve? Did the owner's workload or stress change? Digital Footprint does not control the answer, but it should ask the question — because giving people back time and attention, not just automating tasks, is why the company exists (see 00 Vision.md).

**Level 3 — Business Capability.** Did the business become stronger? Less dependency on individuals, clearer processes, better knowledge management, easier scaling. The methodology does not stop at "the automation works" — it asks whether the business improved.

## 8. The Learning Loop: How Every Client Improves the Methodology

Lessons Learned (`13 Lessons Learned.md`) is the storage mechanism, but AI Prescription requires its own structured reflection stage after every engagement, not just a general note.

**Reflection questions, asked after every engagement:**

Diagnosis — was our initial hypothesis correct? What did we miss?

AI suitability — did the five rejection conditions correctly predict success or failure?

Prioritisation — did our sequencing factors lead to the right order?

Outcome — did expected results happen? What surprised us?

**The process:** Engagement → Reflection → Lessons Learned → Pattern Recognition → Methodology Improvement.

If something repeats, it is promoted (per Governance.md section 4) into one of: AI Prescription improvements (this document), SOP changes (`11 SOP.md`), Automation Catalog updates (`Automation_Catalog.md`), or Philosophy changes (`Company Philosophy.md`) — only when the repeat pattern is truly fundamental, not just operational.

Every client makes this methodology better for the next client. That compounding is the actual long-term asset Digital Footprint is building — not any single automation.
