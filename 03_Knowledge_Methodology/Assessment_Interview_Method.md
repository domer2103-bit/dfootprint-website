# Assessment Interview Method

> **Layer:** Knowledge & Methodology | **Purpose:** The operating method for running the live diagnosis conversation — mindset and technique, not just questions | **Status:** Active — source of truth | **Owner:** Founder | **Version:** 1.0

`Assessment_Interview_Guide.md` supplies the per-hypothesis question templates. This document supplies the method behind using them well — how to think, how to listen, how to press without damaging trust, and how to know when enough evidence exists to stop. AI prepares the ground through `Client_Preparation_Brief.md`. This method is entirely human territory — the consultant owns the diagnosis, start to finish.

## 1. Purpose of the Interview

To discover what's actually happening in the business, not to confirm what the intake suggested. The interview exists to convert hypotheses into either confirmed findings or corrected understanding, using real evidence gathered live. It is not a sales meeting, not a friendly chat, and not a chance to demonstrate expertise by offering quick opinions — every one of those temptations works against the purpose.

## 2. Consultant Mindset

Enter genuinely uncertain about what will be found, even when the Preparation Brief looks compelling — a strong-looking hypothesis on paper has been wrong before (see `Client_Intake_Form_StressTest.md`, Scenario 1's B2/C3 contradiction). Hold curiosity above confirmation: the goal is not to be right about the pre-session hypothesis, it's to find out what's true. Stay comfortable with not knowing yet — resist the pull to form a conclusion halfway through and spend the rest of the session looking for evidence that supports it rather than testing it fairly. Treat the client as the expert on their own business and the consultant as the expert on how to investigate it — neither role should slip into the other.

## 3. Conversation Structure

Open by restating the purpose plainly (see `Assessment_Interview_Guide.md` opening line) — this sets the tone for honest, non-defensive answers from the first minute. Move through each hypothesis from the Preparation Brief in turn, but let genuine new information reorder priorities within the session if it clearly warrants it — the brief is a starting point, not a fixed agenda that overrides what's actually said in the room. Reserve time at the end for open discovery, since the most important friction point is sometimes the one nobody flagged in advance (per `Assessment_Interview_Guide.md`'s Open Discovery step). Close deliberately, not abruptly — see section 8.

## 4. Evidence Gathering Techniques

Ask to see it, not just hear about it — a real quote, a real support inbox, a live screen-share, an actual sample, wherever possible. Description is a starting point; demonstration is evidence. Ask for a specific recent instance rather than a general pattern — "tell me about the last time this happened" surfaces more reliable detail than "does this happen a lot." Count, where countable — how many times last week, how many minutes per instance — turning vague frustration into a number that can later feed `ROI_Calculator.md` honestly. Watch for what isn't said as much as what is — a hypothesis the client visibly avoids or answers briefly may be worth returning to later in the session, once trust has built further. Take contemporaneous notes using the six-field structure from `Client_Conversation_Capture_Template.md` so evidence and interpretation stay separated in real time, not reconstructed from memory afterward.

## 5. Challenging Client Assumptions Respectfully

Challenge the claim, never the person — "help me understand why that happens" lands very differently from "are you sure about that." Use the client's own words back to them as the way in: "you said X, and also Y — help me square those two" is a respectful, evidence-grounded way to raise a contradiction without implying they've been dishonest or careless. Ask permission before pushing on something sensitive — "can I ask a slightly awkward question about that?" — which nearly always gets a yes, and signals respect rather than ambush. Remember the client is usually right about the symptom and sometimes wrong about the cause — validate the frustration as real even while testing whether their explanation for it holds up. If a client becomes defensive, slow down rather than push harder — a defensive client stops producing reliable evidence, and forcing the point at that moment serves the consultant's curiosity more than the diagnosis.

## 6. Verifying Information With Multiple People

Wherever the Preparation Brief's "Who to Include" section identifies a second relevant person, get their account of the same process independently, before comparing it to the first person's version out loud — asking "does that match what your colleague told me" too early can anchor their answer rather than test it. Where only one person is available, say so explicitly in the eventual findings rather than presenting a single-source account with the same confidence as a verified one — per the multi-perspective principle in AI Prescription.md v1.1, a single-perspective diagnosis is a stated limitation, not a silent gap. When two accounts genuinely conflict, that conflict is itself a finding worth including in the report, not something to quietly resolve by picking whichever version sounds more credible.

## 7. What Evidence Is Required Before Making Recommendations

No recommendation is made in the room, ever — recommendations happen afterward, in the analysis stage defined in `11 SOP.md` section 6, deliberately separated from the pressure and rapport of the live conversation. Before a friction point can proceed to that analysis stage, it needs: at least one piece of real evidence beyond the client's initial description (a shown example, a specific counted instance, or a second person's corroborating or conflicting account), an answer to "what happens if nothing changes," and, where a specific solution was requested, a tested understanding of what underlying problem that request was actually meant to solve. A friction point that only has the original intake hypothesis and nothing gathered live is not ready for analysis — it should be logged as insufficiently investigated rather than pushed forward on the strength of the Preparation Brief alone.

## 8. Closing Without Promising an Outcome

State plainly that no conclusions have been reached yet, and that the analysis happens after the session, not during it — repeating the same honesty from the opening, so the client isn't left expecting a verdict before leaving the room. Do not offer reassurance about likely findings ("this looks very fixable," "I think AI will help here") even when it would be kind in the moment — it pre-commits the diagnosis before the analysis stage has actually happened, and undermines the independence the whole Assessment product depends on. Confirm what happens next and when (report and debrief timing, per `11 SOP.md`), and thank the client for their time and openness, particularly if the session required them to be vulnerable about something not working. Leave the client with confidence in the process, not confidence in a particular answer.

## Relationship to Other Documents

This method sits between `Client_Preparation_Brief.md` (what the consultant walks in with) and `11 SOP.md` section 6 (what happens after the session, analytically). `Assessment_Interview_Guide.md` remains the tactical, per-hypothesis question reference used during the session; this document is the craft and judgement that makes those questions actually work in a real conversation with a real, sometimes defensive, sometimes anxious business owner.
