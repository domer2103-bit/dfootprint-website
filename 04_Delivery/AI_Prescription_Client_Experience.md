# AI Prescription — Client Experience

> **Layer:** Delivery | **Purpose:** The service experience from the client's side of the Intake → Brief → Interview → Decision → Report → Debrief chain | **Status:** Active — source of truth | **Owner:** Founder | **Version:** 1.0

This is not marketing copy. It's the design of what a client actually goes through — how the internal methodology chain (`AI Prescription.md`, `11 SOP.md`, and the full template set) should feel from their side of the table. The internal chain defines how the diagnosis is done correctly. This document defines how the client experiences it as clarity, honesty, confidence, and respect, not as jargon, pressure, or a disguised sales process.

## 1. What the Client Is Actually Buying

Not an AI system. Not a promise of automation. The client is buying an honest, evidence-based answer to a question they don't currently have the outside perspective to answer themselves: where is my business actually losing time and capability, and is technology genuinely the right fix, or is it something else entirely. They keep the answer regardless of what it turns out to be, and regardless of whether they hire Digital Footprint for anything further (`AI_Prescription_Assessment_Product.md`).

## 2. Expectations Before Starting

Set explicitly, before payment, not discovered partway through: this is a diagnosis, not a sales conversation. The outcome may include "you don't need AI" or "you don't need us further," and that is a legitimate, valuable result, not a failed engagement. A response takes evidence and time to build properly — it will not be delivered live, in the room, on the day of the conversation. More than one person from the business may be involved, where practical, because a single perspective isn't considered sufficient for an honest answer. The fee is for the diagnosis itself, independent of what happens next.

## 3. Client Journey Stages

Booking and payment → Business Health Check (intake) → Diagnosis session → Waiting period (analysis happens away from the client) → Prescription Report delivered → Debrief call → Decision point (implement, self-implement, do nothing, or seek other help) → Ongoing relationship (or a clean, respectful end).

## 4. What Happens at Each Stage

**Booking and payment.** The client is told plainly, at the point of booking, what they're buying (section 1) and what to expect (section 2) — no surprises introduced later.

**Business Health Check.** A short, natural, low-effort intake (`Client_Intake_Form.md`) completed in the client's own time, in their own words, without needing to prepare or perform. Nothing they say here is treated as a conclusion — they're told this too, so they don't feel judged on a rough first answer.

**Diagnosis session.** A genuine investigation, not an interrogation and not a pitch (`Assessment_Interview_Method.md`). The client is told at the start that no conclusions will be offered in the room, so they aren't left reading tone or body language for hints of a verdict. Where a second person from the business joins, this is framed to the client as thoroughness, not as checking up on them.

**Waiting period.** The client is told clearly how long this will take and why it isn't instant — the separation between conversation and conclusion is part of what makes the eventual answer trustworthy, and it's worth saying so plainly rather than leaving it unexplained.

**Prescription Report delivered.** Written in plain language (`Prescription_Report_Template.md`, `writing-style.md`), organised so the client can see the shape of the whole picture (the roadmap) before the detail of any single finding.

**Debrief call.** A conversation, not a read-through — the client should leave understanding why each recommendation was reached, not just what it says (`Debrief_Call_Structure.md`).

**Decision point.** The client decides, without pressure, what happens next. Digital Footprint states its offer if implementation is wanted, but the decision is entirely theirs, and a "no thank you" is received exactly as gracefully as a "yes."

**Ongoing relationship.** See section 8.

## 5. How Difficult Findings Are Communicated

A difficult finding is one that touches something the client didn't expect — a process that isn't working the way they assumed, a dependency risk they hadn't named out loud, a request of theirs that turned out to be the wrong fix. These are communicated the same way as any other finding: evidence first, then the finding, stated plainly, without either softening it into vagueness or delivering it bluntly without context. The client is never made to feel foolish for not having seen it themselves — the entire point of an outside diagnosis is that habitual problems become invisible from inside a business, and the report should say so if relevant, rather than implying the client should have known.

## 6. How Recommendations Are Presented

Every recommendation — regardless of which of the five outcomes it is (`Prescription_Decision_Framework.md`) — is presented with the same structure and the same confidence: what was found, the evidence for it, and what's recommended. The client is never left wondering why something was or wasn't recommended; the reasoning is always visible, not just the conclusion. Cost and benefit, where relevant, are given honestly and conservatively (`ROI_Calculator.md`), not inflated to make a recommendation more persuasive.

## 7. How "No AI Needed" Outcomes Are Handled

Exactly as confidently and completely as an "implement this" outcome — never as a smaller or apologetic section of the report. The client is told directly why AI isn't the right fit for this specific friction point, using the same evidence-based reasoning as any other finding, and is given a concrete alternative rather than being left with nothing (a process fix, a training recommendation, a referral, or a clear statement of what would need to change before revisiting). Where useful, the client is told this outcome is itself proof the process is honest — the diagnosis was never guaranteed to end in a sale, and this is what that promise actually looks like in practice, not just a line from the marketing.

## 8. How the Relationship Continues After the Assessment

No pressure, no assumed next step. If the client wants implementation, that's scoped and priced as a new, separate conversation, not an automatic continuation. If the client wants to implement recommendations themselves, they're free to do so with the report as their guide, and are told this plainly, without an attempt to make self-implementation feel like a worse choice than hiring Digital Footprint. If the client's answer across the board was "no action needed," the engagement ends cleanly and respectfully, with an open door rather than a chase — Digital Footprint may check in appropriately over time (not immediately, not repeatedly), consistent with playing the long game on trust rather than the short game on this one sale. Every client, regardless of outcome, is a candidate for an honest testimonial about the process itself — the clarity, not just the build — since that's the actual asset this business is trying to prove exists.
